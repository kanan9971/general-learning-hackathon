## Definition
A bond's yield is the annual return you earn if you buy the bond at today's price and hold it to maturity. Price and yield move in opposite directions: when yields rise, existing bond prices fall, and when yields fall, prices rise.

## Intuition
A bond's cash flows (coupons and principal) are fixed when it is issued. If new bonds start paying more, nobody will pay full price for an old bond paying less. Its price must drop until its yield matches what the market now demands.

## Mechanism
A bond's price is the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at the market yield. A higher discount rate makes every future payment worth less today, so the price falls. Because the cash flows cannot change, price is the only thing that can adjust.

## Worked example
A one-year zero-coupon bond pays 100 at maturity. At a 5% yield its price is 100 / 1.05 = 95.24. If market yields rise to 6%, the price becomes 100 / 1.06 = 94.34. The payoff is identical; only the discount rate changed, and the price fell by about 0.9.

## Common misconception
"Higher yields are good for bondholders." Higher yields help people buying bonds from now on, but they cause losses for people already holding bonds, because the market value of their existing bonds falls.

## How it shows up in markets
When a strong economic report pushes Treasury yields up, bond prices and bond funds fall on the same day. Headlines saying "Treasuries sold off" and "yields jumped" describe the same move.

## Interview angle
If asked "yields rose 20bp today, what happened to bond prices?", answer that prices fell, and add that longer-maturity bonds fell more because they are more sensitive to yield changes (duration).
