## Definition
Duration measures how sensitive a bond's price is to changes in its yield. Modified duration approximates the percentage price change for a 1 percentage point change in yield.

## Intuition
The further in the future a bond's cash flows are, the more a change in the discount rate affects their present value. Long-dated bonds therefore move more when yields change.

## Mechanism
Approximate price change ≈ −(modified duration) × (change in yield). The minus sign reflects the inverse price–yield relationship. The approximation works best for small yield moves; for larger moves, convexity makes the true price change slightly more favourable than the straight-line estimate.

## Worked example
A bond with a modified duration of 7 loses roughly 7% of its value if its yield rises by 1 percentage point (100bp), and gains roughly 7% if its yield falls by 1 percentage point. A 25bp rise would cost roughly 7 × 0.25 = 1.75%.

## Common misconception
"Duration is just the bond's maturity." Duration is usually shorter than maturity for coupon bonds, because coupons return some cash earlier. Only a zero-coupon bond has a duration equal to its maturity.

## How it shows up in markets
Long-duration bond funds swing much more than short-duration funds when rates move. The same idea extends to growth stocks, which are often described as "long duration" because much of their value comes from distant cash flows.

## Interview angle
Say which part of the curve moved: a rise in 30-year yields hurts long-duration holdings far more than an equal rise in 2-year yields.
