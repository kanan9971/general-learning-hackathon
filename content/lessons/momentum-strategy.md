## Definition
A momentum strategy buys assets that have performed well recently ("winners") and sells or underweights those that have performed poorly ("losers"). In equities it commonly ranks stocks on roughly the past 12 months of returns, often skipping the most recent month.

## Intuition
Prices can trend because information spreads gradually and investors react slowly at first, then chase recent performance. Trends can persist for months before fading or reversing.

## Mechanism
Academic explanations include underreaction to news (for example, earnings surprises being absorbed slowly), trend-chasing behaviour, and risk-based stories. Momentum profits can also be reduced by trading costs and limited liquidity, especially in small or hard-to-trade stocks.

## Worked example
Qualitative example: after a long market decline, recent "losers" are often the most beaten-down, high-risk stocks. If the market then rebounds sharply, those losers can rally hardest. A portfolio that is short losers and long defensive winners can lose heavily in a short time.

## Common misconception
"Momentum is free money because winners keep winning." Momentum has historically delivered positive average returns, but with rare, severe losses ("momentum crashes"), often in panic periods and sharp market rebounds.

## How it shows up in markets
Momentum-factor ETFs and quant funds can suffer abrupt drawdowns when market leadership flips, for example during violent rotations from recent winners into beaten-down sectors.

## Interview angle
Pair the return story with the risk story: "Momentum has earned a premium over long periods, but it has crash risk tied to market regimes. Some research suggests scaling exposure down when volatility is high."
