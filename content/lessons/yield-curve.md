## Definition
The yield curve plots government bond yields across maturities, from short-term bills to 30-year bonds. Its slope is often summarised by the 2s10s spread: the 10-year yield minus the 2-year yield.

## Intuition
Short-term yields mostly reflect where the central bank is expected to set policy rates soon. Long-term yields also reflect expected growth, inflation over many years, and the extra compensation investors want for locking money up.

## Mechanism
When markets expect the central bank to keep rates high now but cut later (often because growth is expected to slow), short yields can sit above long yields. That is an inverted curve. When growth and inflation expectations rise, long yields tend to rise faster and the curve steepens.

## Worked example
Hypothetical numbers: if the 2-year yield is 4.6% and the 10-year yield is 4.2%, the 2s10s spread is 4.2 − 4.6 = −0.4 percentage points, or −40bp. The curve is inverted.

## Common misconception
"An inverted curve means a recession is certain and imminent." Inversions have often preceded US recessions, but the lag has been long and variable, and the signal is not guaranteed.

## How it shows up in markets
After data that shifts policy expectations, the 2-year yield often moves more than the 10-year. Traders call a curve move "bear flattening" when short yields rise faster than long yields, and "bull steepening" when short yields fall faster.

## Interview angle
Describe both the level and the shape: "Yields rose across the curve, led by the front end, so the curve flattened. That suggests the market priced in a tighter path for policy rates."
