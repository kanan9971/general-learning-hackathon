## Definition
A nominal yield is the stated yield on a bond. A real yield is the yield after adjusting for expected inflation. As an approximation, real yield ≈ nominal yield − expected inflation.

## Intuition
Earning 5% when prices rise 4% a year leaves you only about 1% better off in purchasing power. Real yields measure that true reward for lending money.

## Mechanism
In the US, real yields can be observed from inflation-protected Treasuries (TIPS). The gap between a nominal Treasury yield and a TIPS yield of the same maturity is the breakeven inflation rate, a market-implied estimate of future inflation. A nominal yield can rise because real yields rose, because inflation expectations rose, or both. The two causes have different consequences.

## Worked example
If the 10-year nominal yield is 4.5% and 10-year breakeven inflation is 2.5%, the 10-year real yield is roughly 4.5 − 2.5 = 2.0%. If the nominal yield then rises to 4.7% while breakevens stay at 2.5%, the whole move came from real yields, which rose to about 2.2%.

## Common misconception
"If nominal yields rise, it must be because of inflation." A rise can come entirely from higher real yields, for example when markets expect tighter monetary policy or stronger growth, even if inflation expectations are unchanged.

## How it shows up in markets
Rising real yields raise the discount rate for long-duration assets, which tends to pressure growth stocks. Gold, which pays no income, has often weakened when real yields rise, because the opportunity cost of holding it increases.

## Interview angle
When you explain a yield move, split it: "The 10-year rose, mostly through real yields rather than breakevens, which points to a hawkish repricing more than an inflation scare."
