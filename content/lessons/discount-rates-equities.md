## Definition
A stock's value can be viewed as the present value of the cash flows it is expected to generate. The discount rate used depends on interest rates plus a risk premium. When the discount rate rises, the present value of future cash flows falls.

## Intuition
A dollar far in the future is worth less today, and it is worth even less when rates are higher. Companies whose profits are expected mostly in the distant future ("long-duration" equities, often high-growth tech) are hit hardest when rates rise.

## Mechanism
Higher expected policy rates → higher bond yields (especially real yields) → a higher discount rate → a lower present value of distant earnings → pressure on growth-stock valuations. Near-term cash flows are barely affected, which is why value or cash-rich sectors often hold up better.

## Worked example
The present value of 100 received in 10 years is 100 / 1.08^10 = 46.32 at an 8% discount rate and 100 / 1.09^10 = 42.24 at 9%, a fall of about 8.8%. For 100 received in one year, the same rate change moves the value from 92.59 to 91.74, a fall of only about 0.9%.

## Common misconception
"Rising rates always make stocks fall." Rates often rise because the economy is strong, which can lift earnings expectations. The net effect depends on whether the growth story or the discount-rate effect dominates, and it differs across sectors.

## How it shows up in markets
On days when a hot inflation report lifts yields, the Nasdaq often underperforms and banks may outperform: higher rates can help bank lending margins while hurting long-duration growth names.

## Interview angle
Walk the chain explicitly, then give a counter-case: "Yields rose, so long-duration tech fell through the discount-rate channel. But if earnings guidance had been strong, that could have offset it."
