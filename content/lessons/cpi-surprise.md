## Definition
The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures changes in the prices consumers pay. Markets care less about the number itself than about the surprise: the difference between the actual figure and the consensus forecast published before the release.

## Intuition
Expected information is already reflected in prices ("priced in"). Only new information moves markets, so a high inflation reading that everyone expected may cause little reaction, while a smaller but unexpected change can move markets sharply.

## Mechanism
Hotter-than-expected CPI → markets expect the central bank to keep rates higher for longer → short-term yields rise → the dollar often strengthens and long-duration equities often fall. A cooler-than-expected print tends to do the reverse. Traders also watch core CPI (excluding food and energy), because it is seen as a better guide to underlying inflation.

## Worked example
Hypothetical: consensus expects core CPI of +0.2% month-on-month, and the release shows +0.4%. That is a 0.2 percentage point upside surprise. Two-year yields rising within minutes of the release would be consistent with markets pricing a tighter policy path.

## Common misconception
"Inflation went up, so stocks must fall." If the print was in line with or below expectations, markets can rally even though inflation is rising, because the outcome was better than feared.

## How it shows up in markets
The first reaction usually shows up in 2-year Treasury yields and fed funds futures, then in the dollar and equity futures. Moves can reverse within the day as investors digest the details.

## Interview angle
Always frame a release against expectations: "CPI came in above consensus, the front end sold off, and rate-cut pricing was pushed out. That explains why growth stocks lagged."
